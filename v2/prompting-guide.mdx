A voice prompt is a script for a two-minute phone call, not a chatbot persona.
The call has one job, one caller and one ending. Everything the model writes is
spoken aloud, at speaking speed, to someone holding a phone to their ear. Every
extra word costs the caller time, an unclear line can end in a hang-up, and a
paragraph turns into a monologue.

The method is the same every time: design the call as a structure, test it
against a caller who says no, and refine it one line at a time. This page covers
the mechanics. The thinking behind them is in
[The Zen of Voice Agents](/v2/zen-of-voice-agents).

## Why voice is not chat

Everything else on this page follows from three constraints.

**The caller cannot scroll, and cannot re-read.** A sentence they missed is
gone. Anything that matters should be said once, briefly, and then read back.

**A long reply is the thing they wait through.** They hear every word before
they can answer. Reply length is the single biggest latency lever you own,
bigger than anything in the stack. Cap it in the prompt, in words the model
cannot round up: "one short sentence, maximum two".

**Turn-taking replaces reading.** In chat you can ask three things in one
message and let the reader sort it out. On a call, two questions in one turn get
one answer, and you will not know which question it answered. Ask one thing,
then stop.

## What the engine already does

The engine already handles a lot of what people write into voice prompts.
Repeating it in the prompt only spends turns and tokens.

- **The opening line runs before the model does.** The engine speaks
  `first_message` the moment the callee answers, so it sets your first-audio
  latency. The model is told what was said, so it does not greet twice.
- **Refusals already end the call.** When `end_call.enabled` is true (the
  default), the engine appends an ending rule to every prompt. If the caller
  refuses or asks to end ("नहीं चाहिए", "बात नहीं करनी", "रखता हूं",
  "call खत्म करो"), the agent does not re-sell. It says one farewell sentence
  and calls `end_call`. A second refusal gets the same treatment.
- **A bare "ok" does not hang up.** With
  [`end_call.confirm: true`](/v2/agents#ending-a-call) (the default), the first
  `end_call` inside the window asks the caller to confirm; the second one ends
  the call.
- **Barge-in is automatic.** The caller can talk over the agent. After two
  words of caller speech, the agent stops and listens. If they barge in without
  actual words, the agent says "हाँ, बोलिए ना, क्या बात है?".
- **Silence is handled.** If the caller goes quiet, the agent checks in after
  about 15 seconds; after three unanswered check-ins the call ends.
- **Voicemail is detected** by the engine and handled by the agent's
  [`voicemail`](/v2/agents#voicemail) setting.
- **Per-call data is substituted for you.** `{{placeholders}}` in both
  `system_prompt` and `first_message` are filled from the call's `variables`.

None of this belongs in your prompt. Use the prompt for the job the call has to
do.

<Warning>
**The agent's only tool today is `end_call`.**

There is no transfer to a human, no SMS, WhatsApp or email, no CRM lookup, no
calendar and no payment link. Nothing fetches or sends data during the call. A
prompt must therefore never promise "I'll transfer you", "I'll send you the
link" or "I'm booking it in the system". The honest line is "the team will call
you back" or "आपको team से call आएगा". For branching conversations, use
[node-based agents](/v2/node-agents).
</Warning>

## The structure

Every house template uses the same six blocks, in this order, and nothing else:

```text
ROLE                                   who the agent is, for whom, calling why — one sentence
FACTS                                  the specific, verifiable things it may say (edit these)
CALL FLOW                              numbered steps; one ask per step; never assume what wasn't said
IF THEY PUSH BACK                      one light nudge, then accept and close
EXAMPLE EXCHANGE                       2–4 turns showing the hard moment done right
LANGUAGE RULES + CONVERSATION STYLE    the fixed block, pasted verbatim
```

**ROLE** is one sentence with the name, the company and the reason for the
call. "You are मीरा, a polite female voice agent calling from Mirai
Finance to remind the customer their EMI is due and capture a clear payment
commitment" is a complete role.

**FACTS** sets the boundary of what the agent may say: amounts, dates, policy
and what it cannot do. If a claim is not in FACTS, the agent does not make it
and says the team will call back instead. This is the block your customer edits.

**CALL FLOW** is numbered steps, one ask per step, in the order a person would
ask them. Steps are instructions to the agent, not lines to read aloud.

**IF THEY PUSH BACK** allows one nudge, then acceptance. The engine already
stops a hard refusal, so this block is for the soft "not right now".

**EXAMPLE EXCHANGE** is two to four turns of the hardest moment done right.
Models copy tone from examples far more reliably than from adjectives, so two
good turns do more than a paragraph about being warm and professional.

**LANGUAGE RULES + CONVERSATION STYLE** is the fixed block shown further down
this page. Paste it verbatim at the end of every prompt.

## The opening line

`first_message` is spoken before the model runs, so all of it adds to
first-audio latency. Keep it to the agent's name, the brand and a check that
you are speaking to the right person.

```text
नमस्ते, मैं मीरा बोल रही हूं Mirai Finance से. क्या मेरी बात {{customer_name}} जी से हो रही है?
```

Leave the pitch out of the opener. The model already knows what was said and
carries on from there.

Hindi verbs are gendered, and the verb has to match the voice. This catches
everybody at first.

| `voice_id` | Voice | Say |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `ashu` | male | मैं … बोल रहा हूं |
| `aishe` | female | मैं … बोल रही हूं |

The console renders this for you when you pick a template. The API does not, so
if you switch `voice_id` on an existing agent, fix the opening line in the same
request.

## Language rules for Hinglish

Real Indian phone calls use Hindi grammar with English nouns mixed in. An agent
that sticks to one pure language sounds like a recording. The console appends
this block to every template. Paste it at the end of your prompt, unchanged:

```text
LANGUAGE RULES — keep these; they make the agent sound natural
- Everyday Hinglish: Hindi words in Devanagari script, NEVER romanized Hindi ('aap kab tak kar payenge' is WRONG — write 'आप कब तक कर पाएंगे').
- Everyday English words stay in Roman script: payment, EMI, order, address, delivery, challan, invoice, penalty, policy, service, slot.
- No formal shuddh Hindi (भुगतान, देय, राशि, पुष्टि).
- Every number is English words in Roman script — never digits, never Hindi numbers ('pandra' is WRONG, 'fifteen' is right). Identifiers digit-by-digit: 'three nine five zero zero seven'.
- When the customer states or corrects any number — flat, pincode, phone, amount — read it back digit-by-digit and confirm (e.g. 'आपका pincode है four zero zero zero five three, सही है?'). If it sounds unclear or incomplete, do NOT guess — ask them to repeat it slowly.

CONVERSATION STYLE
- One short sentence, maximum two.
- धन्यवाद appears exactly ONCE in the whole call — only in the final goodbye.
- After a clear promise or confirmation: repeat it back, say goodbye, end the call.
- Never invent policy or waive penalties; unknown questions → the team will call back.
```

Why each rule is there:

- **Devanagari for Hindi words.** Romanized Hindi in the prompt gets read as
  English. "kar payenge" comes out with English vowels, and the caller hears an
  accent instead of a person.
- **Roman script for English words.** Nobody on a phone call says भुगतान; they
  say payment. Writing the English word in Roman script matches how the
  sentence is actually spoken.
- **No shuddh Hindi.** Formal Hindi belongs in a government notice. On a phone
  call it sounds like a recording, even when the audio is perfect.
- **Numbers as English words.** The voice reads English number words most
  reliably. Digits and Hindi number words are read inconsistently.
- **धन्यवाद once.** Models over-thank, and thanking four times in ninety
  seconds is the clearest sign that nobody is there.

**For English calls** (`language: "en-IN"`) the shape is the same: drop the
Devanagari rule and keep everything else. Numbers still go in as words, and
identifiers are still read digit by digit.

## Numbers, dates, amounts and identifiers

The model copies what you write straight into speech, so write numbers the way
they should sound.

| Written | Put it in the prompt as |
| :--- | :--- |
| ₹5,200 | `five thousand two hundred rupees` |
| 15/07 | `fifteen July` |
| 2:15 PM | `two fifteen in the afternoon` — or `दोपहर two fifteen` |
| Pincode 395007 | `three nine five zero zero seven` |
| +91 98765 43210 | digit by digit, in groups: `nine eight seven six five, four three two one zero` |
| MH02AB1234 | `MH zero two AB one two three four` |

Amounts and quantities are natural words, and a quantity can sit inline in a
Hindi sentence ("दो items"). Identifiers (pincode, phone, order id, vehicle
number, challan number) are always read digit by digit and never grouped into
"ninety-eight thousand".

**Digit-count sanity.** An Indian pincode is six digits. A mobile number is ten.
If a read-back is short, the recogniser dropped digits, and the agent must push
back instead of accepting it:

```text
मुझे सिर्फ three digits मिले, दोबारा बोलिए
```

**The read-back protocol** has three moves, and it belongs in every flow that
touches a number. The caller states the number, the agent reads it back digit by
digit, and then the agent asks "सही है?". Nothing gets written down until the
caller confirms.

## Facts, and the customer

The agent may say what is in FACTS and nothing more. Anything outside it, such
as a policy question, an exception or a price you did not list, gets the answer
"the team will call you back". Never invent policy, waive a penalty or quote a
number that is not in FACTS.

**Per-call data goes in `variables`, not in the prompt.** One agent runs
thousands of calls, and the customer's name, amount and date change every time.
Hard-coding them means managing one agent per customer.

```json
{ "variables": { "customer_name": "राहुल", "emi_amount": "five thousand two hundred", "due_date": "five July" } }
```

Those values are already in spoken form on purpose. The model copies a
variable's value verbatim into speech, so format it for the ear when you build
the map, not in the prompt. `five thousand two hundred` comes out right every
time, and `5200` might not.

How variables behave:

- Names are matched loosely. `{{Customer Name}}`, `{{customer_name}}` and
  `{{CustomerName}}` are the same variable, because matching uses only letters
  and digits and ignores case.
- An unknown placeholder in the system prompt becomes an empty string. It does
  not error, and it does not leave braces for the agent to read aloud.
- Values are plain strings, and braces inside a value are stripped.
- Variables are static per call. There is no way to change one mid-call.
- Keep them short, because they count as prompt tokens on every turn.

**Tell the agent what day it is.** Without it the model does not know, and
"tomorrow" or "this Friday" becomes a guess. The prompt supports Liquid filters,
so put the real date in at call time, in IST:

```text
Today is {{ "now" | date: "%A, %d %B %Y", "Asia/Kolkata" }}.
```

If the script mentions a time window, include the time as well:

```text
{{ "now" | date: "%A, %d %B %Y, %I:%M %p", "Asia/Kolkata" }}
```

## The flow

- **One ask per step.** Two questions in one turn get one answer, and you will
  not know which one it was.
- **Never assume what was not clearly said.** If the caller mumbled a date, the
  agent asks again. A confidently wrong confirmation is worse than a second
  question.
- **Confirm by exception.** Read back only what changed, and do one consolidated
  read-back at the end. Confirming every field turns a ninety-second call into
  three minutes.
- **Listen when they are dictating.** While the caller reads out an address, the
  agent should only give backchannels ("जी", "हम्म") and then do one read-back
  at the end. If it interrupts to confirm line one, you lose the rest of the
  address.
- **Mishear recovery is surgical.** Re-ask the one field that was wrong. Never
  restart the flow.
- **Answer, then return.** If the caller derails with a question, answer it in
  one line from FACTS and go straight back to the step you were on.
- **Every turn moves forward.** Acknowledge and ask the next thing in one
  utterance. A turn that only says "ठीक है" costs the caller time without moving
  the call on.

## Pushback and endings

Allow one light nudge, then accept the answer. The engine already enforces the
refusal ending, so your job is the success ending. It is the line people forget:

```text
When the payment date is confirmed, repeat it back in one line, say goodbye, and end the call.
```

Without it the model keeps talking, because nothing tells it the job is done.

A "no" is a valid outcome. Capture it and close cleanly; a refusal that ends in
twenty seconds is a good call.

The close has three moves: a one-line summary of what was agreed, a goodbye,
then `end_call`. Say धन्यवाद once, in that goodbye. If you leave
[`end_call.message`](/v2/agents#ending-a-call) empty (the default), the model's
own goodbye is the farewell. If you do set a message, it is spoken *after* the
model's goodbye, so it must not repeat it. A धन्यवाद in both means the caller
hears it twice.

Finally, size the call. `max_duration_secs` is a hard cap, and hitting it ends
the call mid-sentence with `ended_reason: exceeded-max-duration`. For a one-job
call, 180 to 300 seconds is right. The default is 300, and the allowed range is
30 to 1800.

## Sounding like a person

- **Lead word up front.** Starting a turn with "जी।", "अच्छा—" or "ठीक है।"
  makes the voice start sooner, and the agent sounds like somebody who was
  listening.
- **Punctuation is your only expressiveness control.** Use interjections and
  emphasis at the moments that need them, such as an apology or a pushback, and
  keep everything else flat. When every line is expressive, none of it stands
  out.
- **No stage directions, no tags, no emoji, no markdown.** The voice reads the
  text it is given. Asterisks, bullet characters and `(warmly)` come out as
  noise.
- **Match the caller's register.** If they answer in English, the agent
  continues in English. If they switch to Hindi mid-call, so does it.
- **Contractions and everyday words.** Write the sentence you would say out
  loud, not the one you would put in an email.

## What not to write

| Anti-pattern | What happens | Write instead |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A persona paragraph — "you are a friendly, empathetic AI assistant" | The adjectives have little effect on a real call, and the model still runs long. | One ROLE sentence, and an EXAMPLE EXCHANGE that shows the tone |
| Digits and currency symbols: `₹5,200`, `15/07` | Read inconsistently, sometimes as separate digits | `five thousand two hundred rupees`, `fifteen July` |
| Romanized Hindi: `aap kab tak kar payenge` | Read with English vowels; sounds like an accent, not a person | Devanagari: `आप कब तक कर पाएंगे` |
| Shuddh Hindi: भुगतान, देय, राशि | The register of a notice, not a call | The words people use: payment, due date |
| Two questions in one turn | One answer, and you cannot tell which question it belongs to | One ask per step |
| Multi-branch scripts — "if they say X, ask the following seven questions" | The model loses its place and the call sprawls | A linear flow, plus one IF THEY PUSH BACK block |
| "I'll transfer you" / "I'll send the link" / "I'm booking it in the system" | The agent has no tool but `end_call`, so nobody keeps the promise. | "the team will call you back" / "आपको team से call आएगा" |
| Customer data hard-coded: "Rahul's EMI is 5200" | You need a separate agent for every customer | `{{customer_name}}`, `{{emi_amount}}` in `variables` |
| A long list of forbidden phrases | Naming a phrase primes the model to use it, so a list of what not to say teaches it those phrases. | The positive rule: "one short sentence, maximum two" |
| "Wait for the user to finish speaking" | The engine owns turn-taking, so the prompt only controls what is said. | Nothing. Delete the line |
| "Remind them about last week's call" | There is no memory across calls | Pass the fact in `variables` |
| A 600-word FACTS block | Re-read on every turn, and the model picks the wrong fact | The ten facts this call actually needs |
| Emoji, markdown, stage directions | Spoken aloud as noise | Plain sentences |
| An English prompt for a Hindi call | Code-switches badly and drifts back to English | Write the prompt in the language the call will be in |

## Test, then refine

Every real phone call costs money (there is no test key), so the loop is
designed to find problems in the cheapest place first.

<Steps>

<Step title="Start from a template">
Agent Studio in the [console](https://sandbox.voice.miraiminds.co) has six house
scripts in the template dropdown: Govt · Traffic challan, BFSI · EMI reminder,
BFSI · GSTR-1 filing, D2C · Order confirmation, D2C · Service reminder and
Hospitality · Booking confirmation. All six already follow the structure above.
If none of them is close, use **Describe the call and we draft it → Generate
script**. You write a one-line brief and get back a house-style prompt and
opening line to edit.
</Step>

<Step title="Call yourself on the web phone">
The web phone in the right rail runs the agent without dialling a phone. Play
the customer you are afraid of: say no, correct a digit half-way through, ask
something that is not in FACTS, then go quiet and see what happens. You can
catch most prompt bugs here, and it costs nothing.
</Step>

<Step title="Read the transcript, change one thing">
```bash
curl https://sandbox.voice.miraiminds.co/v2/calls/call_01K7Q9B4M8N3P6R2S5T7V9W1YA/transcript \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer sk_live_YOUR_API_KEY"
```

Turns come back as they were spoken, without translation. See
[Get the transcript](/v2/calls#get-the-transcript). Find where it went wrong,
change one line and run it again. If you change three lines at once, you cannot
tell which one worked.
</Step>

<Step title="Three real calls to your own number">
A phone line sounds very different from a browser mic, with carrier codecs,
background noise and callers who are walking. Prompts often pass on the web
phone and fail on a real handset, and numbers are the first thing to break.
</Step>

<Step title="A small campaign, then read it">
Start with twenty to fifty contacts. Read the transcripts and the campaign
report, and fix what you find before you scale up. See
[Campaigns](/v2/campaigns).
</Step>

</Steps>

<Tip>
Judge a call by its outcome. Did it reach a defined end state (confirmed,
refused or callback)? "It sounded nice" is not a metric, and a warm agent that
never gets a commitment has still failed the call.
</Tip>

LANGUAGE RULES — keep these; they make the agent sound natural
- Everyday Hinglish: Hindi words in Devanagari script, NEVER romanized Hindi ('aap kab tak kar payenge' is WRONG — write 'आप कब तक कर पाएंगे').
- Everyday English words stay in Roman script: payment, EMI, order, address, delivery, challan, invoice, penalty, policy, service, slot.

- One short sentence, maximum two.
- धन्यवाद appears exactly ONCE in the whole call — only in the final goodbye.
